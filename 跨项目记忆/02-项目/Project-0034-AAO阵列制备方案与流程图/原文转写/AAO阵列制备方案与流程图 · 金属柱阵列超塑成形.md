1. Introduction

Due to their unique properties of low

density, high specific surface area, and

efficient electron transport, ordered

metallic nanostructures have been

widely applied in self-cleaning surfaces, [1]

photovoltaics, [2] surface plasmons, [3]

supercapacitors, [4] metamaterials, [5] and

drug delivery, [6] which significantly out-

perform their bulk counterparts. [7] How-

ever, most of the metals exhibit high

melting point, high modulus, and inert

chemical nature (especially for noble

metals), which have raised great dif-

ficulty for the fabrication of ordered

metallic nanostructures. [8,9] Conventional

'top-down' lithography such as e-beam

lithography has been the mainstream for

preparing ordered metallic nanostruc-

tures, but the process is complicated

and time-consuming, which significantly

raises the costs. [10,11] Although tremen-

dous progress has been made via bottom-

up chemical approach, the regularity and

uniformity of the nanostructures are

always compromised due to its high surface energy and com-

plicated particle interactions.

Nanoimprint lithography is a robust and facile technique

for the fabrication of large-scale nanopatterns. [12,13] This tech-

nique has been mainly applied for patterning of polymers and

organic molecules with low modulus and low melting point. To

pattern metals and inorganics with nanoimprint lithography,

one has to use hard molds such as Si or metals [9] with strength

and melting point much higher than those of the imprinted

materials, which has posed big challenges for the fabrication of

large-area Si or metal nanomolds.

Anodic aluminum oxide (AAO), [14] a well-known porous

material possessed with great mechanical strength and thermal

stability, can be facilely prepared by anodic oxidation in large

scale, which makes it an excellent candidate for nanoimprint.

Because of the grain boundaries, fabrication of long-range

ordered AAO nanomolds requires prepatterning of aluminum

sheet before anodizing. [15-20] Thus, dot-like hard stamp (such

as nickel (Ni) or Si) fabricated by focused ion beam or e-beam

lithography has to be applied to prepattern the Al sheets. [21-23]

However, the rough surface of metals and fragile nature of Si

Ordered metallic nanostructures, due to their superior electronic and

photonic properties, have played a vital role in wide range of applications,

such as metamaterials, plasmonic sensing, electrocatalysis, and energy

devices. However, traditional fabrication strategies based on bottom-up self-

assembly and top-down lithography are either poor in uniformity or time-

consuming with low scalability. Here, a robust and cost-effective approach for

the fabrication of highly ordered metallic pillars arrays in centimeter scale is

presented. This is realized by superplastic nanomolding of metals with highly

ordered anodic aluminum oxide templates which are fabricated by the prepat-

terning of aluminum sheets with bulk metallic glass (BMG) mold, followed

by anodizing. The nanopatterning process is rationalized with finite element

simulation to avoid the damage of BMG mold. Finally, it is shown that the

molded metallic sub-micrometer pillars arrays can be used for the surface-

enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) with enhancement factor of ≈10 6 . It is

found that the SERS performance is influenced by the specific surface area of

the pillars in addition to the near-field intensity. This simple and cost-effective

method not only opens new opportunities for rapid prototyping of large-scale

ordered metallic nanostructures for various applications but also provides

guidance for the quantitative analysis on sub-micrometer scale.

As a potential mold material, bulk metallic glasses (BMGs)

exhibit very high strength, hardness, and wear resistance due

to the absence of grain boundaries and dislocations. [31,32] Most

critically, BMGs have a unique property of near net shaping

formability at the supercooled liquid region. Here, we develop a

robust method to fabricate long-range ordered AAO nanomolds

by the prepatterning of aluminum sheet with BMG nanomold

followed by anodizing. By optimizing the prepatterning process

with finite element analysis, the BMG mold can be used repeat-

edly for many times as long as the stress is below the yield

strength in the prepatterning step, which significantly reduces

the cost. Based on the ordered AAO nanomolds, a variety of

ordered metal pillars arrays with tunable aspect ratios were fab-

ricated by the superplastic nanomolding (SPNM) technique, [33]

which is challenging for other strategies. We further show the

use of the molded metal sub-micrometer pillars arrays for the

surface enhanced Raman scattering (SERS). The good con-

trollability and tunability of the ordered metal pillars arrays

decouples the influence of surface area of the substrate and

the near-field intensity on the SERS intensity, which provides

rational guidance on the optimization of SERS configuration

for quantitative analysis on sub-micrometer scale.

2. Results and Discussion

Prior to use, all the Si molds and Al sheets were cleaned with

acetone, alcohol, and deionized water in sonication batch for

5 min. Pt57.5Cu14.7Ni5.3P22.5 BMG (Pt-BMG, with glass transition

temperature of Tg = 509 K and crystallization temperature of

Tx = 598 K) was chosen as a mold material for prepatterning

Al sheets. [34,35] Specifically, Si mold with size of 0.8 × 0.8 cm 2

was pressed against a piece of Pt-BMG at 543 K for 3 min,

the loading stress was ≈100 MPa, ethanol-assisted mechanical

demolding was adopted to peel the Pt-BMG at room tempera-

ture (Figure 1a,b). Then, the prepatterned BMG mold was

pressed onto polished Al sheets with pressure of 50-250 MPa

at room temperature (Figure 1c), which generates ordered

holes in the Al sheets (Figure 1d). The patterned Al sheet was

further immersed in 0.3 m phosphoric acid solution for ano-

dization (140 V, 8 min, 0 °C). [36] The pore size can be tuned

by adjusting the etching time with 5 wt% H3PO4 at 50 °C.

The Al substrate was then removed in a CuCl2/HCl solution

(300 × 10-3 m), which leaves ordered anode aluminum oxide

(AAO) (Figure 1e). Ag pillars arrays were fabricated by super-

plastic nanomolding of Ag disc (with surface area of ≈30 mm 2 )

at 450 °C with the ordered AAO molds and followed by dis-

solving the AAO mold in 3 m KOH solution at 80 °C for ≈3

h (Figure 1f,g). The molding pressure and time were 200 MPa

and 60 s, respectively. The samples were characterized with

SEM after the removal of the residue ions and impurities with

deionized water (Figure 1h).

Because of the great flowability of Pt-BMG at its supercooled

liquid region, Pt-BMG pillars arrays can be replicated with

high molding accuracy, that is, the diameter of the Pt-BMG

pillars is exactly the same as that of the nanoholes in the Si

mold (Figure 2a,b). After compressing the Pt-BMG mold

against an Al sheet at room temperature, the Pt-BMG mold

can be self-demolded with the assistance of ethanol as only

shallow indentations are required to guide the anodizing pro-

cess. The imprinted and un-imprinted regions show distinc-

tive surface features (Figure 2c), which leads to totally different

morphology after anodizing. The imprinted region shows hex-

agonally arranged nanohole arrays as guided by the predefined

nanoholes (left region in Figure 2d), while in the un-imprinted

region, nanoholes with random distribution are observed (right

region in Figure 2d). It is noteworthy that the Pt-BMG mold can

be used repeatedly for many times without damage. Therefore,

prepatterning of Al sheet via nanomolding with robust Pt-BMG

mold provides a practical way to fabricate ordered AAO tem-

plates, which drastically reduces the cost.

holes cannot be generated in Al sheet (Figure 3a). As the

imprinting pressure increases, the depth of nanoholes in the

mold, we carried out finite element simulations (Figure 3b,d,f).

It is clear that for the Pt-BMG mold with pillar size of 230 nm,

the induced maximum stress in the Pt-BMG mold is ≈878 MPa

even under forming pressure of 250 MPa, which is much lower

than its yield strength (1400 MPa, Figure 3h). At this forming

pressure, the experimental results show large-scale nanoholes

arrays can be produced in Al sheets, which is an optimized

condition for the prepatterning of Al sheets with this Pt-BMG

mold (Figure S1a, Supporting Information). For Pt-BMG molds

with different pillar sizes, finite element simulations reveal a

similar trend, that is, the higher the forming pressure is the

deeper the nanoholes are (Figure 3g). By varying the pillar

size and the forming pressure, and monitoring the maximum

stress induced in the Pt-BMG mold, we can obtain the effec-

tive processing region (pink region in Figure 3h), where the

Pt-BMG mold keeps elastic deformation. The effective pro-

cessing region provides the guidance for rational design of suit-

able BMG molds and nanoimprinting conditions. It is noted

that when the forming pressure is high, the imprinted depth

first increases with the pillar size and then decreases, typically

in the case of forming pressure of 200 or 250 MPa (blue and

violet data in Figure 3h, see also Figure S2, Supporting Infor-

mation). However, some of the data points are located in the

yielding strength limited region (gray region in Figure 3h), to

rule out the effect of yielding of the Pt-BMG, we increased the

yield strength of Pt-BMG from 1400 to 3000 MPa in the finite

element analysis and found that even in the elastic deforma-

tion region, the imprinted depth also increases first and then

decreases with the increase of the nanocavity size (Figure S3,

Supporting Information). Therefore, we attribute the pillar size

dependent imprinted depth to the local stress concentration

induced strain hardening. It is needed to point out that even in

the elastic range, damage would occur when the mold is loaded

and unloaded for a long time, which is originated either from

fatigue failure or wear, however, there is no observable dam-

ages after using the same Pt-BMG mold for at least 50 times

(Figure S1b, Supporting Information), which we attribute to

the high hardness (the Vickers hardness of Pt-BMG is HV612,

much larger that of nickel (Ni) stamp, HV274) and great wear

resistance of BMGs. [37]

with varied incubation time (Figure S4, Supporting Infor-

mation). Based on the fabricated AAO templates, a variety of

metals such as Au, Ag, and Au-Ag alloy were molded by the

SPNM technique with excellent uniformity and reproducibility

in large scale (Figure 4 and Figure S5, Supporting Informa-

tion). In the following section, we make use of the molded Ag

sub-micrometer pillars arrays for the application of SERS.

2.1. Discussion

By comparison with other AAO template assisted metal nano-

structures preparation methods, typically such as the widely

adopted electrodeposition method, [39] though our strategy

also includes multiple steps, it can be simply carried out in a

usual universal testing machine equipped with a heating unit

and the whole process is robust and fast, for example, metal

pillars arrays with aspect ratio up to 10 and patterned area up

to 50 mm 2 can be obtained within 10 min (Figure S5, Sup-

porting Information). The direct electrodeposition of metal

pillars arrays using AAO template relies on the prior transfer

of thin AAO template to a flat substrate, which has the gen-

eral challenge of preparing large-area uniform nanostructures

because the transferring process can easily produce defects

such as wrinkles even if very smooth silicon substrate is used.

Meanwhile, the electrodeposition based method also requires

several steps, including preparation of AAO template, thin-

ning of AAO template, deposition of conductive metal (elec-

trodeposition requires template conduction), preparation of

precursor metal salt, and finally deposition of target metal. In

general, the efficiency of electrodeposition is relatively low. It is

noteworthy that both of the electrodeposition method and our

3. Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a facile and cost-effective

strategy to fabricate highly ordered metallic nanoarrays on large

scale with combination of imprinted nanoholes guided anodic

oxidation of Al and the superplastic nanomolding technique. By

varying the experimental parameters along with the finite ele-

ment simulations, an effective processing region in the para-

meter space of forming pressure (or pillar size) and imprinted

depth are established, which provides the guidance for the

low-cost fabrication of long-range ordered AAO templates. In

addition, the good controllability of the proposed method to

pattern Al sheets followed by anodizing with different time

facilitates the investigation of the influence of pillar size on the

Raman intensity besides the local E-field. Considering that the

facile strategy is generally applicable for patterning of different

metals and alloys, this work could not only enable the rational

design and facile fabrication of functional metallic nanostruc-

tures with superior performances but also be of great implica-

tions for a wide range of applications based ordered metallic

nanostructures.

4. Experimental Section

Chemicals and Materials: The Si master mold (hexagonal arrays) with

a period of 350 nm and a hole diameter of 230 nm was purchased from

Beijing GermanTech Co. Ltd. Polished aluminum sheets (with purity

of 99.999% and thickness of 0.2 mm) were purchased from Shenzhen

Topmembranes Technology Co. Ltd. Bulk metals (with purity of 99.999%)

were purchased from Beijing Hezong New Material Technology Co.,

Ltd. The bulk metallic glasses were casted by the vacuum arc smelting

and copper mold casting (WK-I, Physcience Opto-electronics Co., Ltd.,

Beijing). Chemicals (ethanol, acetone, phosphoric acid, hydrochloric

acid, copper chloride, potassium hydroxide) were purchased from

Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Characterization: SEM images were obtained by a scanning electron

microscopy (Zeiss Sigma 500), the accelerating voltage and working

distance were 5 kV and 6.0 mm, respectively. The Raman spectra of

4-mercaptobenzoic acid (4-MBA) molecules absorbed on different

samples were measured with Renishaw Raman microscope system.

The excitation wavelength was 633 nm with power of 55 µW through a

20× objective (Olympus, NA = 0.45). The accumulation time was set to

30 s to gain optimized signal-to-noise ratio.

Electromagnetic Field Simulation and FEA: The finite element

simulation was carried out using the commercial package LS-DYNA,

arbitrary Lagrange-Euler algorithm was used in consideration of

large deformation involved in the SPNM of metal. Ideal elastic-

plastic constitutive law was adopted for BMGs, the Young's modulus,

Poisson's ratio, density, and yield strength of Pt-BMG are 94.8 GPa,

0.42, 15.02 g cm-3 , and 1400 MPa, respectively. [40] The density, Young's

modulus and Poisson's ratio of aluminum were set as 2.7 g cm-3 ,

70 GPa, and 0.33, respectively. [41] Hollomon formula was applied to fit the

experimental stress-strain curve of Al to describe its strain hardening

behavior. [42] 'Mass amplification' method was used to improve the

simulation efficiency without losing the calculation accuracy. Finite

difference time domain simulation of the near field of pillars arrays and

their scattering spectra were carried out with Lumerical. Specifically,

plane wave was chosen as the source and frequency-domain field and

power as the near field monitor. The mesh size is 0.8 nm and periodic

boundary condition was applied for the simulation. The refractive index

of Ag is from Johnson-Christine.
