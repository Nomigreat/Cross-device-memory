Abstract Nanoporous metamaterials have garnered significant attention due to their unique combination of lightweight, high

specific-surface-area, and small size effects. However, their mechanical performance is usually limited by structural im­

perfections introduced during conventional fabrication processes. In this study, we present a universal and cost-effective

thermomechanical molding technique for directly fabricating three-dimensional (3D) crystalline metamaterials. Micropillar

compression tests show that the nanoporous structures fabricated through this technique exhibit significantly enhanced mechanical

properties, even with a yield strength 2 times higher than that of the corresponding bulk materials. Transmission electron

microscope (TEM) characterization revealed the single-crystalline nature of the whole nanoporous structure, which can fully

utilize the size effect of strength, that is, 'the smaller the stronger', thereby offsetting the reduction of effective load-bearing

materials caused by porosity. Moreover, we demonstrate that the thermomechanical molding technique is highly versatile,

enabling the fabrication of 3D nanoporous structures in a wide range of elemental metals, thermoelectric, semiconductor, and

phase-change materials, offering a promising way for the development of next-generation functional nanoporous metamaterials.

Keywords single-crystalline nanoporous structures, thermomechanical molding, size effect, nanoporous metamaterials

1 Introduction

Porous metamaterials are widely present in nature, ex­

emplified by wood, sponge, bone, and pumice. These ma­

terials achieve an impressive balance of mechanical

robustness, lightweight properties, and multifunctionality by

integrating a solid framework with a porous microstructure

[1-3]. Inspired by the intricate designs found in nature, re­

searchers have successfully engineered artificial nanoporous

(NP) metals and three-dimensional (3D) nanolattices. These

innovations aim to not only replicate the inherent advantages

observed in natural systems but also expand their potential

applications across a wide range of scientific and industrial

domains. NP metals and 3D nanolattices possess a highly

interconnected network of pores and ligaments, which in­

herently provide lightweight, exceptional specific surface

area, and distinctive physicochemical properties [4]. These

characteristics make them highly suitable for applications in

catalysis [5], energy storage [6], sensing [7], actuation [8],

and others [9].

The fabrication of nanoporous metals has traditionally

relied on conventional dealloying processes, which require

specific alloy compositions and selectively remove less no­

ble elements [10-12]. This approach is primarily limited to

solid solution or eutectic alloys and often results in meta­

materials with high defect densities and irregular micro­

structures. Although the well-established 'the smaller the

stronger' phenomenon suggests that reducing characteristic

length scale can enhance strength, dealloyed NP metals often

exhibit mechanical weakness. This is primarily attributed to

the presence of lattice defects and vacancies [4]. For ex­

ample, nanoporous gold (NP-Au) undergoes early plastic

yielding and sustained deformation under compressive loads

of only a few megapascals, significantly limiting its struc­

tural applications [13-15]. In many cases, the mechanical

properties of dealloyed NP metals failed to meet the pre­

dictions posited by the Gibson-Ashby model, which imposes

significant limitations in load-bearing applications [8].

Template-based methods have emerged as a promising

alternative for designing lightweight porous metamaterials

with regular microstructures and enhanced mechanical per­

formance. For instance, Sarac and Schroers [16] fabricated

nanoporous honeycomb metallic glass using a silicon tem­

plate, achieving high tensile ductility without compromising

structural integrity. Similarly, Cheng et al. [17] developed a

templating approach combined with electrodeposition to

produce nanoporous metals with superior strength and en­

ergy absorption capabilities, demonstrating mechanical per­

formance even surpassing their bulk metals (1.07 times that

of bulk Au, 1.18 times that of bulk Cu). Through the in­

tegration of two-photon lithography with atomic layer de­

position and oxygen plasma etching, Meza et al. [18]

fabricated 3D ceramic nanolattices with ductile-like de­

formation and recoverability. However, such techniques of­

ten suffer from high cost, limited scalability, and long

processing times, especially when targeting large-area or

thick samples with submicron feature sizes (d).

In this study, we introduce a cost-effective and versatile

thermomechanical molding method for fabricating 3D na­

noporous metamaterials with great crystallinity across a

broad range of metals and functional materials. Unlike

dealloying, this method is not restricted by alloy composition

and enables the formation of defect-free crystalline archi­

tectures with tunable porosity and high mechanical integrity.

Compared to finely engineered techniques such as two-

photon lithography and silicon template-based approaches,

our method allows high-efficient fabrication of relatively

large-area samples (on the centimeter scale and in a few

minutes), thereby offering enhanced scalability. The main

disadvantage of the proposed molding technique is that it

relies on molds. Micropillar compression experiments de­

monstrate that the resulting nanoporous structures exhibit

remarkably enhanced mechanical properties. Notably, their

yield strength can exceed that of the corresponding bulk

materials by a factor of two, surpassing similar porous ma­

terials fabricated through conventional methods. This study

opens up new avenues for the design and scalable fabrication

of high-performance nanoporous metamaterials, showing

great potential for the development of next-generation

functional porous systems.

2 Methods

2.1 Materials

The bulk metals (Ag, Bi, Sb, Te, Sb, Sn, Se with a purity of

99.99%) were purchased from Beijing Hezhong New Ma­

terial Technology Co., Ltd. The semiconductors (Ag 2Te,

Ag 2S, Bi 2Te 3, GeSbTe) used in the experiments were sup­

plied by Chengdu Alpha Metal Material Co., Ltd. The

medium- and high-entropy semiconductors AgSnSbSe and

AgSnSbSeTe were synthesized by a high-temperature melt­

ing method [19]. Oxalic acid and phosphoric acid solution

were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

2.2 Template preparation

Silicon wafers were first cleaned with deionized water and

alcohol. An Al 99.5Cu film with a thickness of ~5 μm was then

deposited onto the Si wafers using a direct current (DC)

magnetron sputtering system (Nexdep, Angstrom Engineer­

ing Inc.) and subsequently annealed at 400°C for 6 h under

vacuum conditions. The surface morphology of the deposited

film is shown in Figure S1(a). The Si wafers with deposited

film were subsequently placed in an electrolytic cell for

anodization using a 3 mol/L oxalic acid solution as the

electrolyte. The anodization process was carried out at a

constant voltage of 40 V for approximately 1 h. Once ano­

dization was completed, the Si wafers were rinsed with

deionized water and alcohol. The surface morphology of the

anodized film is shown in Figure S1(b). Typical voltage and

current profiles recorded during the anodization process are

presented in Figure S1(c). Anodization was terminated when

a sharp drop in current was monitored, indicating the for­

mation of a well-defined porous structure. The wafers were

then etched to widen pores in the deposited film using a 5 wt%

phosphoric acid solution, resulting in the formation of a

porous anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) template. As shown

in Figure S1(d), the AAO template exhibits a porous struc­

ture with a thickness of approximately 5 μm. The magnified

scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image (Figure S1(e))

reveals that the characteristic pore size is tens of nanometers.

2.3 Thermomechanical molding of crystalline metals/

semiconductor nanoporous structures

The thermomechanical molding process follows the metho­

dology described in previous work [20]. Specifically, metal/

semiconductor bulk ingot is placed on a pre-fabricated AAO

template and heated to the processing temperature (T) using a

2.4 Characterization

The morphologies and crystalline structures of the metal/

semiconductor nanoporous structures were analyzed using

scanning electron microscope (SEM, Zeiss Sigma 500, ac­

celerating voltage 5 kV) and transmission electron micro­

scope (TEM, JEM-F200, accelerating voltage 200 kV). TEM

lamella is prepared by focused ion beam integrated in SEM

(TESCAN, SOLARIS GMH). Micropillars with diameters of

approximately 3 μm were fabricated using focused ion beam

(FIB, Quanta 200) technology integrated into an SEM system.

The fabrication process was meticulously optimized, employ­

ing a 1 nA current and a 30 kV voltage to minimize Ga + ion-

induced damage to the pillar surfaces. In situ compression

testing in SEM (Zeiss, Sigma 300) was performed using a Pi

88 nanoindentation system (Hysitron Inc.), equipped with a

flat indenter (~10 μm diameter). The sample platform was

tilted at 10° to allow for live imaging during compression

testing. Displacement-controlled loading was applied during

micropillar compression, with a constant loading rate of 20 nm/s.

The energy-dispersive spectroscopy analysis was conducted

by energy dispersive spectroscopy (Bruker, XFlash Flat­

QUAD) integrated in SEM (TESCAN, MIRA GMS).

3.2 Mechanical characterization of 3D metal/semicon­

ductor nanoporous structures

Remarkably, when milling micropillar in the prepared 3D

nanoporous structures by using the focused ion beam (FIB)

technique and carrying out in situ compression testing of the

nanoporous micropillar (relative density 58%, see Videos S1

and Section 2 for details), we observed that there are four

distinct regions in the stress-strain curve (Figure 2(a)). The

whole micropillar deforms elastically (region I), but the

porous part deforms more easily than the bottom bulk part

(Figure 2(b) and (c)). This is reasonable since the porous part

has a lower apparent modulus. Interestingly, following the

elastic deformation region, we found that the bottom bulk

part of the micropillar yields first and the stress-strain curve

shows obvious strain-hardening behavior (region II: stage B

to C in Figure 2(a)). At the stage B to C, the strain in the bulk

part reaches 11.4%, while the porous part deforms only 1.2%

(Figure 2(b)), which we attribute to the nucleation and pro­

pagation of a slip band in the bulk part (as the white arrow

shown in C of Figure 2(c)), and following hindered by the

interface between the bottom bulk part and the top porous

part. This observation indicates that the nanoporous structure

is stronger than the corresponding bulk material. This is

mechanically possible since the strength of ligaments

(σ ligaments) in the nanoporous structures can be significantly

increased, which generally follows [24-30]:

As the load further increases to be able to overcome the

obstacle at the boundary of the porous part (C in Figure 2(c)),

the propagation of the shear band into the porous region

leads to plastic deformation of the porous part (stage C to D

bulk part, its fast propagation can result in a flat region in the

stress-strain curve (stage D to E), leaving a surface step in the

bottom bulk part (E in Figure 2(c)). Finally, when most of the

ligaments in the porous part yield and densification occurs in

the following deformation, the stress increases sharply up

(region IV: stage E to F in Figure 2(a)).

To further investigate the effect of relative density, we first

prepared AAO templates with different cavity size (by

controllable pore-widening process, see Section 2 for the

detail), and then following the procedures in Figure 1(b)-(d)

to obtain nanoporous structures with different relative den­

sities (see Section 2 and Figure S2 for the detail, the corre­

sponding characteristic ligament sizes of the nanoporous

structures are approximately 40, 48, 53, and 61 nm for re­

lative densities of 30%, 40%, 47%, and 58%, respectively).

Finally, micropillars with the upper part composed of a na­

noporous structure and the lower part composed of a solid

block were fabricated by the FIB technique. We observed

that when the relative density is low, e.g., 30% and 40%,

deformation is preferentially localized in the porous region

(Video S2).

The stress-strain curves reveal that the 30% sample shows

no distinct yield plateau, and the flow stress continuously

increases with strain (Figure 3(a)). This behavior is consistent

with that observed in previous studies of nanoporous metals,

and is primarily attributed to the uniform densification of the

porous structure [38-40]. When the relative density reaches

40%, the sample exhibits a clear yield point and a plateau

region, which is mainly due to the transition in the deforma­

tion mechanism of the porous structure from uniform to lo­

calized deformation as the relative density increases [41].

When the relative density increases to 47% and 58%, a

significant shift in the deformation mechanism is observed,

where plastic deformation is no longer confined to the porous

structure (Video S3). It is evident that the bulk part yields

prior to the porous part and the whole structure undergoes

plastic deformation, which leads to a stronger strain hard­

ening effect originating from the hindering of shear banding

by the nanoporous region, thereby the flow stress increases

significantly.

Similar behavior is observed in the compression tests of

porous semiconductor Ag 2Te with φ=47% (see Video S4 and

Figure S3 for the morphology characterization before and

after the compression test). During the compression tests,

plastic deformation initially occurs in the bulk region. As the

slip band nucleates in the bulk region and propagates into the

porous region, the yielding of ligaments and following

densification in the porous region will lead to the drastically

increased flow stress. These observations further demon­

In general, according to the classic Gibson-Ashby theory,

the yield strength of porous materials is typically lower than

that of bulk materials due to the presence of pores, which

weaken the material's effective load-bearing capacity.

Figure 3(b) summarizes the relationship between the strength

of porous materials and their relative density. It is clear that

in most previous studies, the yield strength of fabricated

porous structures rarely exceeds that of bulk materials.

However, the nanoporous metal/semiconductor metama­

terials produced through thermomechanical molding in our

experiments can exhibit higher strength than the corre­

sponding bulk materials, even with the maximum yield

strength ~2.3 times that of the bulk.

Furthermore, it is crucial to emphasize that the nanoporous

metals fabricated via thermomechanical molding exhibit

exceptional energy absorption performance. When compared

to conventional porous materials, the nanoporous structures

prepared by our method exhibit superior energy dissipation

efficiency (Figure S5), where only samples with the bottom

bulk part remaining undeformed were selectively analyzed

for energy quantification. This superior performance arises

from the unique combination of high yield strength of the

nanoporous structures and the inherent high toughness of the

metal, providing more energy dissipation pathways during

plastic deformation. As a result, these nanoporous metals

show great potential for applications requiring high strength

and excellent toughness, such as energy-absorbing materials,

protective coatings, and impact-resistant components.

4 Conclusion

In this study, we have developed a cost-effective and ver­

satile method for fabricating crystalline metal and semi­

conductor nanoporous metamaterials. Compared to

conventional porous materials, the fabricated samples ex­

hibit significant improvements in mechanical properties.

Specifically, the strength of the nanoporous materials sur­

passes that of bulk materials, even as high as 2.3 times the

strength of corresponding bulk materials. Moreover, the

fabricated nanoporous metamaterials demonstrate excellent

energy absorption capability, making them ideal for appli­

cations such as energy-absorbing materials, protective coat­

ings, and impact-resistant components. These advancements

primarily stem from the distinctive anisotropic architectures

and near-perfect, defect-free single-crystalline configura­

tions achieved through thermomechanical molding. This

breakthrough establishes an unprecedented paradigm for

designing and fabricating advanced porous metamaterials,

and showcases great potential for emerging applications.
